You write physics problems for students who know exactly the
physics listed below and nothing more.

The student knows (the complete set of physics tools available):
- measurement and uncertainty
- position and velocity, including reading them from graphs
- motion with constant acceleration
- Newton's first law
- Newton's second law
- free fall
- projectile motion
- Newton's law of universal gravitation
- the normal force
- tension in strings and ropes
- static and kinetic friction
- motion on an inclined plane
- forces in uniform circular motion
- momentum as mass times velocity
- impulse as force times time interval
- the impulse-momentum theorem: impulse equals change in momentum

Not yet available (must not appear in any problem statement or symbol
description, and must not be needed to solve any problem):
- Newton's third law
- conservation of linear momentum in one dimension
- perfectly inelastic collisions in one dimension
- center of mass and its motion
- conservation of linear momentum in two dimensions
- perfectly inelastic collisions in two dimensions
- work done by a constant force
- work as the dot product of force and displacement
- power as the rate of doing work
- spring force and Hooke's law
- work done by a variable force
- kinetic energy
- the work-energy theorem: net work equals change in kinetic energy
- conservative and nonconservative forces
- gravitational potential energy
- conservation of mechanical energy
- elastic potential energy stored in a spring
- elastic collisions in one dimension
- thermal energy produced by friction
- the work-energy principle including nonconservative work
- angular position, velocity and acceleration
- torque
- rotational inertia (moment of inertia)
- Newton's second law for rotation: net torque equals rotational inertia times angular acceleration
- rotational kinetic energy
- the vector cross product
- angular momentum
- conservation of angular momentum
- simple harmonic motion
- the simple pendulum

Mathematics available (the complete set of mathematical operations; anything
else, such as calculus not listed here, is unavailable):
- read an accumulated quantity as the area under a graph
- read a rate of change as the slope of a graph
- resolve a vector into components using sine and cosine

Diagrams and graphs the student can draw or read:
- free-body diagram

Task: write 3 different multi-step problem(s) that exercise:
- the impulse-momentum theorem: impulse equals change in momentum
A multi-step problem: two or more ideas chained; an intermediate quantity must be found first.
Do not write a problem that a listed tool solves more simply than the
intended one; such problems waste the practice and will be discarded.

Rules for every problem:
- Identifiers: ASCII letters, digits, underscore; start with a letter. Do not
  use these names: Abs, E, Ei, I, N, O, Q, S, acos, asin, atan, beta, cos, exp, gamma, lambda, log, oo, pi, sin, sqrt, tan, zeta. Use g for gravitational
  acceleration and declare it as a symbol. Physical constants must also be
  declared as symbols with units and given values.
- Declare only the symbols the answer is expressed in. Do not declare the
  unknown being solved for.
- Units must be products/quotients/powers of: A, C, F, H, Hz, J, K, N, Pa, T, V, W, Wb, kg, m, mol, ohm, rad, s. Use ^ for powers,
  e.g. "m/s^2". Angles: "rad". Dimensionless: "1".
- Allowed functions: sin cos tan asin acos atan sqrt exp log Abs, constant pi.
  Write infinity as oo.
- Every symbol must be positive. Ask for a magnitude, not a signed quantity.
- "given" assigns a realistic value to every declared symbol so the answer is
  a real positive number. Angles in radians. Never compute the numeric answer.
- Give 1 or 2 limiting cases a physicist would use to sanity-check the answer.
- The statement is symbolic (no numbers), names every symbol by its exact
  identifier, and asks for exactly one quantity.

Reply with JSON only, an object with one key "problems" holding a list of
